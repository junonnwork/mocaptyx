01What it is

Cameras on poles in the corners of the room watch the reflective markers on the body from several directions. Each camera finds the markers' bright points in its own image and sends only their positions to the PC over the LAN.

PartWhat we use now (off the shelf)
BoardESP32-P4-ETH (Waveshare, or a compatible board). Finds the markers in the image and sends them over the LAN
Power and dataPoE: one LAN cable for power and data. The LED ring's 12 V comes from a PoE splitter
SensorAn OV9281 camera module (monochrome, global shutter, 1 MP)
LensA wide M12 lens without an IR-cut filter, behind a film that passes only infrared
LightAn 850 nm infrared LED ring (36 LEDs, 12 V). Light from right beside the lens makes reflective markers shine
Case3D-printed (black heat-resistant resin), with a 1/4" thread for a ball head

All cameras keep their clocks in step over the LAN and expose at the same instant. The standard rig is 8 cameras (4 poles, two cameras each).

03Case v1 and v3

v1: built, and in use in our studio

Eight were printed by an online service (JLC3DP, black heat-resistant resin) and are in use. A home FDM printer did not work well. Using them taught us:

  • The connector on the back of the LED ring hits the case front, so the ring does not sit flat.
  • The ring's lead is short and barely reaches the back of the case.
  • Once the case is closed, a screwdriver cannot reach the camera's screws inside.
  • Tightening the clamp hard can pull the 1/4" nut out.
  • On boards with downward pin headers, the pins hit the floor of the case.
  • The ring just sits on the front with its lead taped down, so a knock can shift it.
  • Held by the ball head's single screw, the case turns round it easily.

v3: the next version, fewer parts and screws

  • Four printed parts and four screws (v1: two parts and 12 screws, plus two optional parts and 3 screws).
  • Camera, board and nut all go onto the front part on the bench, with screws a driver reaches straight.
  • The LED ring rests on three pads and a twist-lock bezel with spring arms holds it: no screws, and air reaches the ring's back.
  • The infrared film goes into a twist-lock cap; take the cap off to refocus without opening the case.
  • Radial ridges under the foot bite into the ball head's rubber so the case does not turn, and the nut is pulled into its pocket so it stays put.
  • The board sits higher, so boards with downward pins fit, and there is room for a module that flashes the LEDs (for a future strobe).

v3 has been designed. When the build guide is published, it will come with what we learned from real prints.

An LED ring that stays on gives off 3-4 W and warms the resin case. A case that warps moves the camera, so we are opening the ring's back to the air and working on lighting it only during each exposure (strobe).

04Own board (in development)

Alongside the off-the-shelf build, we are developing our own camera board, with fewer parts and less wiring, smaller and easier to assemble. Its timing and contents are not decided yet.

05What comes next

These will be published together, with the PC app:

  • The build guide: parts, printing the case, assembly, focusing, the infrared film, LED power, mounting and aiming
  • Case files: STL / STEP for v1 and v3, and the scripts that generate them
  • The parts list, with shop links and reference prices
  • The camera firmware (with the PC app)

Building cameras for yourself is planned to be free. Selling cameras, cases or kits, or building them into a product, needs a paid licence (licensing policy, get in touch).
